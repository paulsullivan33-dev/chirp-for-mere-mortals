# Limits

What CHIRP cannot do, and what it
must not be asked to do — the chapter
the rest of the guide has been
promising.

## Your radio may simply not be in it

CHIRP's 564 models (the lab's count)
are each there because somebody
reverse-engineered that radio's
memory and wrote a **driver**. New
models wait, sometimes for years, for
that somebody; a few never get one;
a few brands encrypt or lock their
programming path and stay out on
purpose. There is no generic mode
that pours a plan into an unknown
radio. If your model is absent,
chapter 12's neighbours are the
answer, and the supported-radios
page — not hope — is the authority.

## CHIRP programs; it does not control

When the upload ends, CHIRP's
relationship with the radio ends.
It does not steer the radio live,
log what you hear, decode digital
voice, or record. A **live-mode**
radio (the minority, driven one
memory at a time) narrows even that:
no whole image, so no whole-image
backup or verify, and each edit is
its own small risk. Radio control,
logging and decoding are real tools
with their own guides; they are not
hiding inside CHIRP.

## One mistake programs every channel

The clone model is all-or-nothing,
and nothingness cuts both ways. A
plan with a wrong tone in one row
gives a radio one silent channel. A
plan built on the *wrong base image*,
or uploaded to the *wrong radio*,
gives a radio a hundred surprises at
once — settings included (chapter
9). There is no partial credit and
no undo button beyond chapter 5's
golden rule. This is why the guide
makes such a fuss about backups,
working copies and verify passes:
they are not ceremony, they are the
whole safety net, and they are made
of nothing but files and habits.

## The licence is not in the software

CHIRP knows frequencies, not
privileges. It will write a channel
on any frequency the radio's memory
physically holds, including:

- bands your licence does not cover
  (a General-class channel plan and
  a Technician's radio are different
  objects);
- services with their own rules —
  FRS, GMRS, MURS, marine — where
  the law asks for a **certified**
  radio, and an amateur handheld
  programmed to the right frequencies
  is still the wrong radio;
- public-safety and commercial
  channels that arrive inside a
  stranger's image (chapter 5's
  biography problem), where
  transmitting is not a grey area at
  all.

Listening widely is lawful in many
places and is a hobby of its own.
Transmitting is the act the licence
governs. **A channel you may only
listen to belongs in your plan
marked receive-only or not at all** —
and the check belongs to the human,
because the software has no way to
do it for you.

## A cloned image carries its owner with it

An image is a whole personality:
channels, yes, and also settings, and
on digital radios identities — a
radio ID, a call sign burned into
codeplugs, talkgroup lists. Cloning
a friend's image to get their
channels also copies their identity
and their mistakes, and transmitting
as their ID is a real harm, not a
favour returned. Chapter 6's CSV is
the clean way to share: frequencies,
tones and names travel; identity
stays home. If you accept an image
from anybody, read it the chapter 5
way — every line — before it goes
near a radio of yours.

## The last limit is the radio's trust in you

An interrupted upload can leave a
radio with a half-written memory.
Most recover by simply uploading
again; a few need the maker's own
recovery path, and a very few can be
bricked by bad luck at the wrong
byte. The defence is chapter 10's
checklist — charged battery, seated
cable, the right file — applied like
it matters, because once in a career
it will.
