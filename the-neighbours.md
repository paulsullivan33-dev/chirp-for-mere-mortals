# The Neighbours

What else can put frequencies into
a radio, and how to choose honestly.
These comparisons are characterisations
from each tool's documentation and
reputation, not benchmarks — the lab
ran CHIRP, not its neighbours.

## The maker's CPS

Every serious radio ships with its
maker's own **CPS** (customer
programming software): Kenwood's,
Icom's, Yaesu's, Motorola's, one
program per model or family, usually
Windows-only, sometimes free and
sometimes not. What the CPS does that
CHIRP does not:

- **Everything the radio can do.**
  Firmware updates, the deepest
  settings, the features the maker
  never documented for outsiders —
  including, on some models, settings
  CHIRP's reverse engineering has not
  reached.
- **Authority.** If a radio misbehaves
  under warranty, "programmed with
  the maker's software" is a sentence
  that ends arguments.

What CHIRP does that the CPS does
not: speak to the *other* radios.
One CPS per maker per era is a
drawer of cables, passwords and
Windows versions; one CHIRP is one
way of working for the whole shelf,
one CSV format for every plan, and a
scriptable command line underneath.
The honest household answer is often
both: the CPS owns firmware day,
CHIRP owns channel day.

## The front panel

The radio itself is the zero-software
option: its keypad and menus program
every channel it owns, slowly. The
front panel wins when there is one
channel to change, no computer in
reach, and a repeater to add *now*,
from the summit or the roadside —
which is why chapter 6's plan is
never a substitute for knowing your
own radio's manual path to a new
memory. It loses at everything the
clone model is for: bulk entry,
checking, sharing, restoring, and
noticing your own typo before it
becomes a silence.

## RT Systems

**RT Systems** sells programming
software per radio family, and its
pitch is polish: a tidy spreadsheet
interface, repeater data built into
the purchase, cables that simply
work, and support you can telephone.
For a club equipping volunteers who
will program radios but will never
read a guide like this one, that
polish is a real argument. What it
is not is broader or more capable
than CHIRP at the core job — and it
is one more paid program per radio
family, on Windows (and some Mac),
where CHIRP is one free program for
nearly all of them, on everything.
The choice is temperament and
budget, not correctness.

## The spreadsheet itself

Worth naming as a neighbour: chapter
6's plan lives in a spreadsheet
program, and a spreadsheet alone —
no CHIRP — is where many clubs keep
the *master* list, arguing about it
in the open before anything touches
a radio. That is a workflow CHIRP
is designed to join (its CSV *is*
the spreadsheet) rather than a rival
to it. The plan of record in a file,
the radios as readers: whoever keeps
that discipline can change tools
mid-stream and lose nothing.
